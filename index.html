Buying to rent out

A long-term play. You profit a little every month, plus loan paydown and appreciation over time.

Screening rules

  • 1% rule. Monthly rent should be at least 1% of the purchase price. A $200,000 house should rent for $2,000 or more. With mortgage rates around 7%, hitting exactly 1% often only breaks even, so many investors look for 1.2–1.5%.
  • 50% rule. Expect about half the rent to go to expenses other than the mortgage: taxes, insurance, repairs, vacancy, capital reserves and management. The other half has to cover the mortgage and your profit.
  • Cash flow minimum. Many investors want at least $100–$300 per door per month after every expense and reserve. Aiming for $500 is the conservative, safer end.

Return targets

  • Cap rate 6–8%+ in most markets. Higher usually means more risk or a rougher area.
  • Cash-on-cash return 8–12%+ on the money you actually put in.
  • DSCR 1.2–1.25+. Net operating income should be at least 1.2–1.25 times the mortgage payment. Many investor loans require this.

Monthly expenses to budget

  • Vacancy: 5–8% of rent
  • Repairs: 5–10% of rent
  • Capital reserves (roof, furnace, water heater): 5–10% of rent
  • Property management: 8–10% of rent plus leasing fees. Run the numbers with it even if you plan to self-manage.
  • Landlord insurance, property taxes, any utilities you pay (water, sewer and trash are common in multi-family), HOA and rental license fees.

Habits that protect you

  • Base rent on actual comparable rentals (Zillow, Rentometer, local listings, property managers), not the seller's numbers.
  • Always budget reserves, even if the house is in great shape.
  • Keep 3–6 months of expenses per property in cash.
  • Buy where tenants want to live: near jobs, hospitals, transit and decent schools.
  • Make your profit when you buy, by paying the right price. Don't count on appreciation to rescue a deal that loses money each month.
ExampleA $190,000 house renting for $2,500 passes the 1% rule at 1.32%. With 25% down at 8%, the mortgage is about $1,046. Taxes, insurance and reserves bring total costs to about $1,950, leaving roughly $550 a month in cash flow.

Fix and flip

A short-term play. You profit once, when the house sells. Speed and accurate budgets matter most.

The 70% rule

Maximum offer = (ARV × 70%) − rehab cost

ARV is the after-repair value, what the house will sell for once it's fixed up. The 30% cushion covers closing costs, loan costs, holding costs, selling costs and your profit. In expensive or very competitive markets some flippers stretch to 75–80%, which leaves a thinner margin. In a slowing market, stick to 70% or lower.

ExampleARV $260,000 and rehab $45,000: $260,000 × 70% = $182,000, minus $45,000 = a $137,000 maximum offer.

Profit targets

  • At least 10–15% of the ARV, or about $25,000–$30,000 net per flip. Less than that can disappear with one surprise.
  • 20%+ return on the cash you put in.

Budget rules

  • Rehab contingency of 10–20%. Older homes, common across the Lehigh Valley, often hide knob-and-tube wiring, old plumbing, foundation issues, asbestos or lead paint.
  • Holding costs of about 1–2% of the purchase price per month: loan interest, taxes, insurance and utilities. Every extra month eats profit.
  • Selling costs of about 6–10% of the sale price: agent commissions, transfer tax and seller concessions.
  • Buying costs of about 2–4% of the purchase price, plus loan points (often 1–3% on hard-money loans).

Habits that protect you

  • Base the ARV on homes that actually sold within about half a mile in the last 3–6 months. Ignore list prices.
  • Get contractor bids and a professional inspection before you buy.
  • Don't over-improve. Finish to the neighborhood's level. Kitchens, bathrooms, paint, flooring and curb appeal pay back best.
  • Watch the clock. Most profitable flips are finished and sold within 4–6 months.
  • Have a backup plan. Know what the house would rent for, so you can hold it as a rental if it doesn't sell.

The BRRRR method

Many investors combine both strategies: Buy a distressed house, Rehab it, Rent it out, Refinance based on the new value to pull most of your cash back out, then Repeat with the next property. Use the Fix & Flip tab to check the rehab, then the Rental tab to check the rent.

Due diligence checklist

What to gather before making an offer.

Income

  • Market rent from comparable units
  • Current leases, rent roll, deposits held and payment history
  • Other income: parking, laundry, storage

Expenses

  • The actual property tax bill
  • A landlord insurance quote
  • Utilities the owner pays
  • Rental license and inspection fees
  • HOA dues, if any

Financing

  • Investment-loan rate (usually 0.5–1 point above owner-occupied)
  • Down payment, closing costs and transfer tax
  • Points and terms for hard-money loans

The property

  • Age of the roof, furnace or boiler, water heater, electrical panel, plumbing and windows
  • Professional inspection, sewer scope and radon test
  • Lead paint disclosure for homes built before 1978
  • Legal unit count, zoning and permits
  • Open permits or code violations
  • Flood zone, oil tank, septic or well issues

The location

  • Rental vacancy and days on market
  • Who rents there: students, families, workers
  • Crime, school district, jobs and transit
  • New development or supply coming

Your exit

  • Appreciation trends in the area
  • Tax benefits (ask a CPA)
  • Whether you could sell, refinance or rent it instead

Pennsylvania notes

State rules and local quirks that affect the numbers. Confirm details with a PA real estate attorney or accountant.

  • Property taxes combine county, municipal and school district taxes. The school tax is usually the largest piece. Allentown-area taxes are fairly heavy, often around 2–2.5% of value per year.
  • Realty transfer tax is 1% to the state plus a local share, which is higher in some cities. It's usually split between buyer and seller.
  • Reassessment risk. A high sale price can lead the school district to appeal your assessment and raise your taxes. Some districts do this actively.
  • Security deposits (Landlord and Tenant Act of 1951) are capped at 2 months' rent in the first year and 1 month after that. Deposits over $100 held more than 2 years must earn interest, and unused deposits must be returned within 30 days.
  • Evictions go through the local magisterial district judge.
  • Rental licensing and inspections are required in Allentown, Bethlehem, Reading, Philadelphia and many other towns.
  • Older housing stock. Radon is common in eastern PA, and pre-1978 homes need lead disclosures. Some cities also have lead-safe rental rules.
  • LLC or not. Whether to hold properties in an LLC is a question for a PA attorney and your accountant.
